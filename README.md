# CryptoPortfolioGuide

Building a crypto portfolio: allocation, rebalancing, and risk management. A portfolio is not a collection of coins you like — it is a deliberate plan for how much risk you take, where, and what you will do when prices move.

> This guide is educational only, not financial advice. Crypto is highly volatile, and any portfolio can lose most or all of its value.

## 1. Start with the whole picture, not the coins

Before choosing any crypto, answer:

1. **What is this money for?** Long-term holding, learning, or speculation are different jobs with different risk budgets. Do not mix them in one pot without realising it.
2. **What can you afford to lose?** Crypto should be money whose complete loss would not damage your rent, food, debt payments, or emergency fund. If losing it would hurt badly, it does not belong in crypto.
3. **How long can you leave it alone?** Money you need next month should not be in volatile assets. Time horizon determines how much volatility you can survive.
4. **How will you react to a 50% fall?** This is not hypothetical in crypto — it happens regularly. If your honest answer is panic-selling, your allocation is too aggressive, whatever the spreadsheet says.

Write these answers down. They are your portfolio's constitution, and you will need them when markets test you.

## 2. Allocation: the core decision

**Allocation** means what percentage of your crypto funds goes to each asset or category. It matters more than picking individual coins, because it controls your overall risk.

A common beginner structure (illustrative, not a recommendation):

| Category | Role | Illustrative range |
|---|---|---|
| Large, established coins (BTC, ETH) | Core: most liquid, longest history | 50–80% of crypto allocation |
| Other established projects | Satellite: higher risk, higher potential | 10–30% |
| Stablecoins | Dry powder and stability: funds ready to deploy, or a defensive position | 5–20% |
| Experimental / new projects | Speculation: money you fully expect you might lose | 0–10% |

Adjust to your risk tolerance, not to hype. A portfolio that is 100% in a single new token is not a portfolio — it is a bet.

**Across all your money, not just crypto:** decide what percentage of your total savings belongs in crypto at all. Many advisers suggest a small single-digit percentage for most people. Whatever you choose, decide it deliberately, not by accident after a series of purchases.

## 3. Diversification: what it does and does not do

Diversification means spreading across assets whose prices do not always move together, so one failure does not take everything.

What it does:

- Reduces single-project risk: a hack, scam, or failure in one token hurts less.
- Smooths volatility somewhat, if assets are genuinely different.

What it does not do:

- Protect you in a market-wide crash. In crypto crashes, almost everything falls together — correlations spike toward 1 exactly when you most want diversification to work.
- Help if your "diversified" portfolio is 20 versions of the same bet (for example, 20 meme coins, or 20 tokens on the same chain with the same risks).

**True diversification** in crypto includes: different chains, different sectors (store of value, smart contracts, DeFi, infrastructure), different risk levels, and — most importantly — assets outside crypto entirely (cash, savings, traditional investments).

## 4. Position sizing

Position sizing is how much you put into each asset. Rules that keep beginners alive:

1. **No single position should be able to ruin you.** If one token going to zero would be devastating, it is too large.
2. **Size by conviction and risk, not by hope.** Higher conviction and lower risk justify larger sizes; a new, unproven token justifies a tiny size, however exciting it looks.
3. **Use percentages, not amounts, when comparing.** "$1,000 in Token A" means different things in a $2,000 portfolio and a $100,000 portfolio.
4. **Revisit size after big moves.** A position that doubles becomes a larger share of your portfolio — and a larger risk — even if you did nothing. That is what rebalancing addresses.

## 5. Rebalancing: selling winners, buying losers — deliberately

**Rebalancing** means periodically restoring your target allocations.

Example: your target is 60% BTC, 40% ETH. After a rally, you hold 75% BTC and 25% ETH. Rebalancing means selling some BTC and buying ETH until you are back to 60/40.

Why do it:

- It forces you to sell high and buy low, mechanically, without emotion.
- It prevents a single winner from silently becoming most of your risk.
- It keeps your portfolio's risk profile where you intended it, not where the market dragged it.

Practical choices:

- **Calendar rebalancing:** rebalance monthly or quarterly, regardless of moves. Simple, disciplined.
- **Threshold rebalancing:** rebalance only when an allocation drifts by a set amount (for example, 5 or 10 percentage points). Fewer trades, fewer fees.
- **Fee awareness:** every rebalance costs trading fees, spreads, and possibly tax (in many countries, swapping is taxable). For small portfolios, frequent rebalancing can cost more than it saves. Rebalance less often, or only on large drifts.

## 6. Risk management for the whole portfolio

- **Emergency fund first.** Before crypto, hold cash for emergencies. Crypto is not an emergency fund — it can fall 50% the week you need it.
- **No leverage for beginners.** Borrowed money and leveraged tokens amplify losses and can liquidate your whole position. Spot holdings cannot be liquidated; leveraged ones can.
- **Separate wallets by purpose.** A long-term holding wallet you rarely touch, a trading wallet with smaller amounts, and an experimentation wallet with tiny amounts. This separation prevents a mistake in one area from touching everything.
- **Keep records.** Every purchase, sale, swap, and reward, with dates and values, for tax and for reviewing your own decisions (see CryptoTaxBasics).
- **Plan for crashes in advance.** Write down what you will do if your portfolio falls 30%, 50%, 70%. Decide while calm, so you do not decide while panicking.

## 7. Reviewing your portfolio: a quarterly routine

Every quarter, ask:

1. **Did my allocations drift?** Rebalance if they drifted beyond my threshold.
2. **Did any thesis break?** If you bought a token because of a specific reason (a product, a team, a use case), is that reason still true? If not, the position's reason for existing is gone, whatever the price did.
3. **Did I add risk without noticing?** New approvals, new protocols, bridged assets, and staked positions all add risk that does not show in a simple balance.
4. **Are my backups and security still good?** Seed phrases, 2FA, approvals, and device security — check them on the same schedule as your portfolio.
5. **What did I learn?** Review your trading journal and your mistakes. A portfolio review without a lessons review wastes the most valuable part.

## 8. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| All-in on one coin | Cap any single position; core holdings first |
| Chasing every new narrative | A written allocation plan, changed deliberately, not impulsively |
| Never rebalancing | Calendar or threshold rebalancing, fee-aware |
| Rebalancing too often | Fewer, larger rebalances; count fees and taxes |
| Counting unrealised gains as wealth | Only realised, withdrawn value is spent; paper gains can vanish |
| Investing money needed soon | Match time horizon to volatility; short-term money stays out of crypto |
| Ignoring taxes on rebalancing | Swaps are taxable in many countries — factor tax into rebalancing decisions |
| Measuring success against hype | Measure against your own plan and risk tolerance, not against someone else's gains |

## 9. A simple starter portfolio process

1. Decide your total crypto budget (money you can afford to lose).
2. Decide your core/satellite/stable split, in percentages, and write it down.
3. Buy the core first, in small steps, with test withdrawals to your own wallet.
4. Add satellites only after you can explain each project's tokenomics, risks, and reason for existing in plain language.
5. Keep a stablecoin reserve for opportunities and for rebalancing — not as "cash doing nothing", but as deliberate dry powder.
6. Set a rebalancing rule (calendar or threshold) and a review date.
7. Review quarterly, rebalance on rule, and record everything.

If you cannot complete step 4 for a token, do not buy it yet. Understanding is a prerequisite, not a luxury.

## 10. Frequently asked questions

**How many coins should I hold?**
Fewer than you think. Each position needs research, monitoring, and security attention. For most beginners, a handful of well-understood positions beats twenty half-understood ones.

**Should I hold stablecoins in a portfolio?**
Yes, usually — as dry powder, as a defensive position, and as a way to rebalance without leaving crypto. Remember stablecoins carry issuer and depeg risk (see StablecoinsExplained).

**Is dollar-cost averaging (DCA) better than lump sum?**
DCA (buying fixed amounts regularly) reduces timing risk and emotional decisions, and suits beginners. Lump sum can outperform in rising markets but risks buying everything at a peak. DCA's main benefit is behavioural: it keeps you investing steadily instead of trying to time the market.

**When should I sell everything?**
Only for reasons decided in advance: your thesis broke, you need the money, your risk tolerance changed, or your time horizon shortened. "The market fell" alone is not a plan — falls are normal in crypto, and selling everything in a panic locks in losses.
